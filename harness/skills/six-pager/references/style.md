# Style

## Why narrative

Bezos's 2004 email to the senior team that ended slide decks: "Well structured, narrative text is what we're after rather than just text. If someone builds a list of bullet points in word, that would be just as bad as powerpoint." The reason given: "the narrative structure of a good memo forces better thought and better understanding of what's more important than what." Every rule below serves that: the reader should see what matters most, how things connect, and why the conclusion follows.

## Rules and their reasons

**Lead each paragraph with its point.** The first sentence states the claim; the rest supports it. Readers skim topic sentences on a second pass and in discussion ("on page three you say...").

**Numbers instead of adjectives.** "Margin fell from 31% to 26% over six quarters", not "margins are under pressure". An adjective asks the reader to trust you; a number lets them judge. Give the comparison (vs plan, prior period, competitor or alternative), because a number alone has no meaning.

**Then the so-what.** After the number, say what it means for the decision. "Tickets per 1,000 accounts rose 18% while accounts grew 6%, so volume is outgrowing the customer base and hiring alone will not close the gap." A fact without consequence is appendix material.

**No weasel words.** Words nobody can verify: significantly, substantially, very, extremely, nearly, many, several, various, numerous, robust, seamless, world-class, best-in-class, leverage (as a verb), synergy, should help, could potentially, it is believed. Replace with the number or delete. "Approximately" is fine when followed by a number.

**Short sentences.** Keep most under 30 words, average under 20. One idea per sentence.

**Plain words, defined terms.** No team jargon or acronyms without definition on first use. Write for a smart reader from another organization.

**Name who.** "Support ops will..." not "it will be ensured that...". Active voice; owners for every commitment.

**Own the bad news.** A miss gets the same prose as a win: what happened, the cause, what changed. Hiding it in the appendix is the fastest way to lose the room.

**Argue against yourself.** State the strongest objection in the form its best advocate would use, then answer it. If you cannot answer it, the recommendation is not ready.

**Prose, not slides in paragraphs.** A paragraph made of short fragments joined by commas is a bullet list in disguise. Sentences should connect with because, so, but, which means.

**Bullets only where content is a list:** goals, tenets, appendix. Tables are good for numbers that compare (targets vs actuals, option costs). Charts belong in the appendix with a sentence in the body stating what they show.

**Round to the precision the decision needs.** "$6.94M budget cap", not "$6,937,843"; "about 1,840 tickets", not "1,838.4". The ledger keeps exact values; the prose carries two or three significant figures unless an exact figure is the point (a contract price, a headcount). False precision reads as a spreadsheet pasted into prose and hides which digits matter.

**Precise dates and units.** "By 30 June 2027", "per month", "USD". Not "soon", "next half", "a lot".

**Answers in four kinds:** yes, no, a number, or "we don't know, and here is how we will find out". Unknowns stated plainly read stronger than hedges.

## Before and after

Weak: "Our support team made significant progress this year, substantially improving responsiveness while dealing with many challenges around growth."

Strong: "Median first response fell from 9.5 to 6.1 hours, short of our 4-hour goal. Volume grew 41% against a 15% plan because the March billing release generated 11,200 tickets, and we spent Q2 hiring to catch up rather than on the self-service work that would have cut volume."

Weak tenet: "We put customers first."

Strong tenet: "When a fix and a deflection compete for the same engineer, we choose the fix, because a deflected ticket type returns and a fixed one does not."

## Length

About 550 words per page at 11-point single spacing. Body at most 3,300 words (six pages, a ceiling); aim for 1,500-2,500. Keep the appendix short: a computations summary that points to the ledger, input conflicts, and an FAQ of at most five hard questions, each item referenced from the body or answering a likely question.
